Moving from Miro

Bring your boards.
Leave the 296 MB.

Velm is a whiteboard written in Rust. One process instead of fourteen, 18.5 MB instead of 296 — and it holds 100 fps on a hundred-thousand-item board, because it only ever draws what is actually on your screen. Copy a board out of Miro and paste it in; the pen strokes come too.

How these were measured
  • Install size — du -sk on Miro's application bundle against the one this repository's build script produces. The same thing you would download.
  • Memory — ps -o rss, both sampled after the app had settled with a real 596-widget board open and every item drawn. Velm is one process, so that is the whole story; Miro's figure sums all fourteen of its.
  • Frame rate — Velm's own HUD, averaged over a 45-second run.

Reproduce the frame figures yourself: ./target/release/vellum-app --bench 100000 --hud --exit-after 25

Before anything else

What comes across, and what you give up.

The honest half first. Velm does less than Miro does, on purpose and by accident, and the list below is the accurate one — it is cheaper to read it now than to find it out in a fortnight.

What comes across

On the 596-widget board all of this was measured against, 596 of 596 widgets import and 532 of them arrive losslessly.

  • Sticky notes, text, frames and connectors

    With their colours, their styling and their positions — including the parent-relative coordinates Miro stores for 485 of those 596 widgets.

  • Every pen stroke

    All 219 of them on that board, as real ink you can erase, move and redraw — not as a flattened picture.

  • Link cards, with their pictures

    The site, the title, the blurb and the preview image, in the same three card sizes Miro draws them in.

  • Images at full resolution

    Point the Mac app at a .rtb backup and it recovers the original pixels rather than a thumbnail.

  • The 64 that arrive degraded

    Mostly embeds. They arrive as a card carrying the title, the link and the provider; the live frame inside them is not imported.

What you give up

Some of this is unfinished and some of it was cut deliberately. Which is which is said each time, because they are not the same promise.

  • Working on a board with other people, live

    No cursors, no presence, no comments, no voting — on the Mac either. Cut on purpose: this is a single-player tool. It is the biggest thing on this list, and the first one to check against how you actually work.

  • Templates

    There is no gallery and no starter set. Unfinished rather than refused.

  • Phone and tablet apps

    The browser client works on a tablet, with real pinch and pan. There is no app in either store.

  • Windows, and Intel Macs

    Apple Silicon is the supported platform. Velm has never been built or tested on Windows; an Intel Mac builds from source, with no published binary.

  • Embeds that play in the board

    A video link becomes a card with its poster and a ▶ that opens your browser. A live embed needs a browser engine per card — 60 to 150 MB idle, each — and it could not zoom, be hidden by a frame, or export.

  • PDF pages

    A PDF's bytes are stored and kept safe; nothing draws its pages yet.

Bringing them over

Moving a board is three keystrokes.

There is nothing to export, no file to convert and no connector to authorise. These are the steps as they appear on your boards page.

  1. Copy it in Miro

    Open the board and press ⌘A, then ⌘C.

  2. Paste it in Velm

    Click the box on your boards page and press ⌘V. Nothing is sent anywhere until you press the button.

  3. Name it

    Give it a name and press Bring it across. It arrives as a new board; nothing you already have is touched.

Miro's own API cannot give your pen strokes back.

On the board all of this was measured against, Miro's own REST API lists 577 items — connectors come from a separate endpoint, which is why that total and the 596 above differ — and 310 of them, 54%, come back with no content at all: a position, and isSupported: false. That is every one of its 219 pen strokes and all 91 of its link cards, the two largest things on it, while the response still counts them and looks complete. Miro's clipboard carries them in full. That is why Velm reads the clipboard rather than the API.

Pictures are the one exception. The clipboard carries references to images rather than the pixels themselves, so a board pasted into a browser tab arrives complete apart from those and tells you exactly how many are missing. The Mac app reads the originals out of a .rtb backup, which a browser tab cannot open.

Once you are here

What you get.

The canvas

Everything a board is made of

41 shapes, connectors that stay attached to what they join, sticky notes, text, images, frames, tables, kanban boards, mind maps and charts.

Drawing

A pen that behaves like one

Stroke smoothing, an eraser that rubs out strokes or whole objects, and undo that groups a typed word into a single step rather than one per letter.

Placing things

Snapping that suggests

Alignment and equal-spacing guides that are loose rather than strict, and hold ⌘ to suspend them mid-drag. Snap-to-grid is there too, switched off.

Anywhere

Your Mac, and any browser

The desktop app and this server draw the same board with the same renderer, and an edit made in one appears in the other without a reload.

Organising

A trash that keeps things

Boards, folders, and the ones you star. Deleting a board puts it in Recently deleted, where it stays until you say otherwise — nothing here expires anything, ever.

Getting work out

PNG, PDF, SVG and CSV

From the Mac app. A browser tab exports SVG and CSV, and searches every word on the board you have open — inside tables and cards included.

The server you are looking at

In a browser, on hardware you own.

The same renderer, compiled to WebAssembly and drawing through WebGPU. Measured in Brave on an M2 against a 1,306-item board, served from a copy.

155 ms

Cold start to a drawn frame, GPU handshake and board fetch included.

9.4 ms

Steady-state frame time on a fitted board. Opening one is slower, and that is where the work is.

681 KB

Over the wire on the first load — 4.6 MB of WebAssembly, compressed.

What a tab cannot do

Said here rather than found out later.

  • Pictures export as placeholders

    An SVG downloaded here carries every item's geometry, style and words, but each picture comes out as an empty rectangle — the browser hands an image straight to the graphics driver, so there are no bytes left for an exporter to write. You are told how many. CSV is unaffected; PNG and PDF are desktop only.

  • A .rtb backup cannot be attached here

    So a board pasted into a tab arrives without its image pixels, and says how many are missing. The Mac app is what reads the archive.

  • Nothing here runs a program on your machine

    A browser tab has no subprocesses. That is structural rather than unfinished.

Free, and yours. MIT or Apache-2.0, at your option. There is no paid tier, no seat count, no trial and no account with us — because there is no us. Your boards are files in a folder, on your own machine or on a server you run. Nothing is uploaded anywhere else, and the board of record is always the server's rather than the tab's, so a browser that gets closed never holds the only copy.

Two things that are honestly untested. The server has been run on macOS only — nothing in it is platform-specific and it is meant for Linux, but "meant for" is not "measured on". And nothing has yet been served over HTTPS from a real domain; every figure above is from a loopback address.
